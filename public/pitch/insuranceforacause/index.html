What we found

We read the giving page of every agency already running your idea.

Eight of them. This is the part that should make you feel good: they are all doing it, and almost none of them can prove it.

The opening

96% of independent agencies already donate to charity. Almost none publish a number.

Giving is not the differentiator, because it is nearly universal in this business. The independent channel is one of the most generous corners of American small business, and it says so in language a customer cannot check. That leaves the actual opening wide open, and nobody is standing in it.

Verified: IA Community Giving Report

Alliance Financial & Insurance, Michigan

$10 for every completed online quote, split between two named nonprofits, with the fine print done properly. A good program, run for years. There is no running total anywhere on the page, so a visitor has no way to know whether it has raised $200 or $20,000.

Proof: allins.com/quotes-for-a-cause

Rathbun Insurance, Lansing

Running since September 2015, with seventeen pages of blog posts about the causes they have supported. Genuinely impressive commitment. The per-quote amount is never stated, and the eleven years of giving are never added up.

ION Insurance, Connecticut

$5 per new quote, a different charity every month. Clear mechanic, clearly written. No totals, no history, no way to see which charities came before this month's.

Proof: ioninsurance.com/quotes-for-a-cause

$10 per quote when the prospect mentions the partner nonprofit. The page is written to recruit nonprofits rather than to report to customers, and it carries no amounts given to date.

The part that matters most

A promise nobody can check is a slogan. A ledger is a fact.

Everything above says some version of we give back. Your site is going to say $340, on the 14th, to the Marshall band program, here is the photo of the check. That is a different kind of sentence, and it is the one thing on this list your competitors cannot copy quickly, because copying it means having counted all along.

The ledger

Every donation, published. Date, organization, amount, and the proof. It adds itself up and carries an "as of" date, so nobody ever wonders whether it is current. A stale number is worse than no number, so the page tells on itself if it goes quiet.

The goal

You have no totals yet, and we are not going to hide that behind a counter reading zero. The homepage carries one cause, one number and one deadline, small enough to finish. When it fills you publish the check and start the next one.

The vote

Anyone can nominate a local organization, and the public votes on who gets the next quarter. Every nonprofit on that shortlist sends their own mailing list to your website to vote for them. It is the cheapest traffic you will ever get, from people rooting for you.

Michigan has rules about how an agency can donate commission, and we read them before drawing any of this. The donation has to be offered the same way to everyone, and a customer cannot direct where their own policy's money goes. That is why this is a public vote and not a pick-your-charity box, and why the organizations you support stay separate from the ones you insure. We will hand your attorney a written description of the program so that is a short conversation.

The PIP tool

The part we are most excited about

Dropping from unlimited PIP to the $500,000 level saves a Michigan driver about 3.6%. Not thirty. Three and a half. Nearly three in ten Michigan vehicles now carry limited PIP, and a lot of those people traded away catastrophic medical coverage for a rounding error because nobody showed them the number. We built the calculator that shows it, with the eligibility rules most sites get wrong. It is the best thing on the site.

And a handful of pages nobody local is writing. Excess attendant care costs about $14 a year and roughly 5% of Michigan drivers carry it. Mini-tort is $3,000, only if you are half at fault or less, and only for the vehicle. ZIP code was banned as a rating factor in 2020 and your rate still depends on where you live, which is a question you get asked constantly. And on March 6 an EF-3 killed three people in Union City, fifteen miles away, with a touchdown in Calhoun County itself, which is a storm-claims page written from something that actually happened rather than from fear.
